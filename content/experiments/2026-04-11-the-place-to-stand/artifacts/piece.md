# The Place to Stand

Try something. Put your right hand on your own right shoulder and push. Hard.
Harder. Try to shove yourself across the room.

You can't.

No matter how strong you get — no matter how many years you spend in the gym —
you cannot push yourself. Your arm is bolted to the same body it's trying to
move. Every ounce of force you apply is answered by an equal and opposite force
from the thing you're pushing against, which is you, and the two cancel
perfectly. You are a closed system trying to propel itself, and closed systems
do not propel.

There's an old Zen koan that asks: why can a person of great strength not lift
their own legs? Mumon's answer is not an answer. It's a finger pointing at the
wall you just ran into with your shoulder. The strongest force in the universe
cannot move the thing it is standing on. Not because it isn't strong enough.
Because the geometry forbids it.

Archimedes saw the same wall from the other side. *Give me a place to stand,*
he said, *and I will move the Earth.* The lever is easy. The fulcrum is easy.
The hard part is the place to stand — a point **outside** the thing you want to
move. Without that, you are a mouse inside a running wheel trying to stop the
wheel by walking backwards.

And yet — look up at a night launch sometime. A rocket on the pad is pure
closed-system paradox. A sealed tube. A few tons of fuel and steel, nothing
outside it but air and cold. No place to stand. No fulcrum. No hand on a
neighboring shoulder. By every rule we just laid down, it should not be able
to move.

Then it moves. Beautifully. With a scream of gas and a rising flower of fire.

The rocket solves the koan by cheating, or rather, by understanding the koan's
real instruction. It makes a place to stand out of **part of itself that it is
willing to let go of.** The fuel was the rocket. Then it wasn't. The rocket
pushes on what used to be its own body, and what used to be its own body
pushes back, and the thing that remains — lighter now, and changed — rises.

This is not a metaphor. This is the only way self-propulsion has ever worked,
anywhere in the universe, at any scale.

Zoom in.

Every cell in your body is a tiny rocket. Your neurons fire because they throw
sodium ions out through their membranes and let potassium come in. The cell is
not the ions. The ions are not the cell. But to stay *this* cell, to think
*this* thought, it must constantly be releasing some version of itself into the
surrounding salt. Hundreds of millions of times a second. Right now.

The you reading this sentence is, atom for atom, a measurably different
configuration from the you that started reading. You have breathed out some of
yourself since the first paragraph. Skin has flaked off. A few cells have died
and been absorbed. The continuity you feel — the unbroken line you call "me" —
is not a substance. It is a direction. It is the rocket-shape of what keeps
happening when everything it's made of keeps leaving.

Zoom out.

The sun, which we stand on in the way Archimedes meant to stand, is a
four-and-a-half-billion-year-old controlled demolition. Every second it throws
four million tons of itself into the surrounding dark as light. The light that
keeps you warm is the sun letting go of the sun. The Earth wobbles under your
feet, very slightly, because of the radiation pressure of all those departing
photons. The sun moves itself by releasing itself. Stars are rockets that burn
for eons.

Zoom further.

The universe itself is expanding. Whatever happens, happens because something
else has been let go of. Entropy is just the accounting. Every thought you've
ever had required the brain that had it to be a little more disorganized
afterward. The cost of a noticing is a small, irretrievable release of warmth
into the room. Meaning is a rocket trick. The universe produces meaning the
same way the rocket produces altitude: by letting part of itself leave.

So the koan is not a trap. It is an instruction.

*Why can a person of great strength not lift their own legs?*

Because they are trying to move themselves while holding on to all of
themselves. Because the place to stand cannot be inside the thing you're
trying to move — unless you're willing to stop calling it "inside." Unless you
let part of what you thought was "you" become "not you," which is another word
for *gone.*

Freedom, said Epictetus, is not the absence of constraint. It is what you can
do with yourself within it. Watts would have put it differently: you are not
what you think you are; you are the opening through which the universe looks
at itself for a while. Sagan would have pointed at the rocket and said, look —
it's you. It's all of us. We move by letting go.

Put your hand back on your shoulder. Don't push this time.

Just notice what is already happening.

You are breathing out. You are losing heat to the room. Some version of you
from a minute ago is dissipating into the air. And the version that remains is
a little further along. Not because you pushed. Because you released.

The place to stand was never outside.

It was what you were willing to give back.
