Try something. Put your right hand on your own right shoulder and push. Hard. Harder. Try to shove yourself across the room.

You can't.

No matter how strong you get, you cannot push yourself. Your arm is bolted to the same body it's trying to move. Every ounce of force you apply is answered by an equal and opposite force from the thing you're pushing against... which is you. And the two cancel perfectly. (breath)

There's an old Zen koan that asks: why can a person of great strength not lift their own legs? The answer isn't really an answer. It's a finger pointing at the wall you just ran into with your shoulder. The strongest force in the universe cannot move the thing it is standing on. Not because it isn't strong enough. Because the geometry forbids it.

Archimedes saw the same wall from the other side. Give me a place to stand, he said, and I will move the Earth. The lever is easy. The fulcrum is easy. The hard part is the place to stand... a point outside the thing you want to move. (long-break)

And yet... look up at a night launch sometime. A rocket on the pad is pure closed-system paradox. A sealed tube. A few tons of fuel and steel, nothing outside it but air and cold. By every rule we just laid down, it should not be able to move.

Then it moves. Beautifully. With a scream of gas and a rising flower of fire.

The rocket solves the koan by cheating... or rather, by understanding the koan's real instruction. It makes a place to stand out of part of itself that it is willing to let go of. The fuel was the rocket. Then it wasn't. The rocket pushes on what used to be its own body, and what used to be its own body pushes back. And the thing that remains, lighter now and changed, rises. (breath)

This is not a metaphor. This is the only way self-propulsion has ever worked, anywhere in the universe, at any scale.

Every cell in your body is a tiny rocket. Your neurons fire because they throw sodium ions out through their membranes. To stay this cell, to think this thought, the cell must constantly be releasing some version of itself. Hundreds of millions of times a second. Right now.

The you reading this is, atom for atom, a different configuration from the you that started reading. You have breathed out some of yourself since the first sentence. The continuity you feel — the unbroken line you call me — is not a substance. It is a direction. It is the rocket-shape of what keeps happening when everything it's made of keeps leaving. (long-break)

The sun is a four-and-a-half-billion-year-old controlled demolition. Every second it throws four million tons of itself into the dark... as light. The light that keeps you warm is the sun letting go of the sun. Stars are rockets that burn for eons.

And the universe itself is expanding. Whatever happens, happens because something else has been let go of. Entropy is just the accounting. Every thought you've ever had required the brain that had it to be a little more disorganized afterward. Meaning is a rocket trick. The universe produces meaning the same way a rocket produces altitude: by letting part of itself leave. (breath)

So the koan is not a trap. It's an instruction.

Why can a person of great strength not lift their own legs?

Because they are trying to move themselves while holding on to all of themselves. Because the place to stand cannot be inside the thing you're trying to move... unless you are willing to stop calling it inside. Unless you let part of what you thought was you become not you... which is another word for gone. (long-break)

Put your hand back on your shoulder. Don't push this time.

Just notice what is already happening.

You are breathing out. You are losing heat to the room. Some version of you from a minute ago is dissipating into the air. And the version that remains is a little further along. Not because you pushed. Because you released.

The place to stand was never outside.

It was what you were willing to give back.
