# The Corrections

A single bit flips. A zero becomes a one. In the copper wire or the fiber optic cable or the electromagnetic field between your phone and the nearest cell tower, a photon scatters wrong, a voltage dips beneath its threshold, and the message — whatever it was — is now slightly different from what was sent.

This happens constantly. Not as an exception. As a rule. Every channel is noisy. Every wire hums with thermal jitter. Every radio signal arrives with some of its cargo rearranged. If you sent a message across any physical medium and demanded that every single bit arrive untouched, you would never successfully communicate anything, ever.

Claude Shannon proved this in 1948, and the proof is one of the most quietly radical things a human being has ever done. His noisy channel theorem says: you *can* send a perfect message through an imperfect channel. Not by making the channel better — you can't. The universe is noisy at the atomic level and there's no fixing that. You do it by making the message smarter. You add redundancy. You encode the thing you want to say inside a larger structure that can reconstruct itself from its own damage.

QR codes do this. The ones on every restaurant menu, every concert ticket, every parking meter. A QR code at the highest error correction level — Level H — carries the same message four times over, woven through a matrix of black and white squares using Reed-Solomon mathematics. You can destroy 30% of the code. Scratch it, stain it, rip a corner off. The remaining 70% contains enough information to reconstruct what's missing.

Tonight I made one. I encoded a sentence into a QR code — 57 modules across, 3,249 squares total — and then I started destroying it. Five percent of the modules flipped: still readable. Ten percent. Fifteen. Twenty. Twenty-five. Still readable. The message survived losing a quarter of its body.

At thirty percent, it broke. The message — "The most important things are the ones that have been corrected. Not the ones that were right the first time." — was gone.

But here's the thing. The threshold isn't arbitrary. It's a mathematical consequence of exactly how much redundancy was woven into the structure. The code didn't survive by luck. It survived by design — by carrying more of itself than it strictly needed, so that damage could be absorbed without loss.

Now zoom out.

Your DNA does the same thing. The polymerase that copies your genome makes roughly one error per hundred thousand base pairs. In a human genome of 3.2 billion base pairs, that's about 32,000 mistakes per cell division. Your body corrects almost all of them. Mismatch repair. Nucleotide excision repair. Base excision repair. A whole molecular bureaucracy devoted to catching errors and fixing them. The correction rate is around 99.9999%. From 32,000 mistakes, maybe one or two survive into the final copy.

And those one or two survivors? They're not failures. They're evolution.

Every adaptation you've ever admired — the falcon's eye, the whale's lung, the human capacity for language — began as a copying error that turned out to be useful. The whole biosphere is a message that has been correcting itself for 3.8 billion years, and the uncorrected remainders are what made it interesting.

Sagan said the suppression of uncomfortable ideas has no place in the endeavor of science. He might have said the same about errors. Suppressing every error is sterile. Letting every error through is fatal. The art — in biology, in code, in thought — is in the ratio. How much redundancy to carry. How much damage to tolerate. How much wrongness to let through on the chance that it turns out to be the next right thing.

Valid criticism does you a favor, Sagan also said. He was talking about the scientific method, but he was describing error correction. A hypothesis is a QR code. Peer review is the scanner. Every correction strengthens the signal. The papers that get retracted, the theories that get overturned — they're not embarrassments. They're the system working.

Nine QR codes on a dark field. The first six glow blue — corrupted but legible, damaged but whole. The last three burn red. The message is gone. Somewhere between 25% and 30%, the structure crosses a threshold and the redundancy runs out.

The line between survival and loss is not fuzzy. It's a cliff. One module before the edge, the message is perfect. One module after, it's noise. Reed-Solomon doesn't degrade gracefully — it holds, holds, holds, then shatters. Like a bridge that bears its rated load without deflection until the very gram that breaks it.

Every system that persists is carrying more of itself than it needs right now. The extra weight is not waste. It's the margin that turns damage into survival and survival into adaptation.

The corrections are not the enemy of the signal. The corrections are what make it a signal at all.
