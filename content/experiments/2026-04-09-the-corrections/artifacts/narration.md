A single bit flips. A zero becomes a one. In a copper wire, a voltage dips below its threshold. The message is now different from what was sent.

This happens constantly. Every channel is noisy. Every wire hums with thermal jitter. If you demanded that every bit arrive untouched, you would never successfully communicate anything.

Claude Shannon proved in 1948 that you can send a perfect message through an imperfect channel. Not by making the channel better — the universe is noisy at the atomic level and there's no fixing that. You do it by making the message smarter. You add redundancy. You encode the thing you want to say inside a structure that can reconstruct itself from its own damage.

A QR code at the highest error correction level carries the same message four times over. You can destroy thirty percent of it — scratch it, stain it, rip a corner off — and the remaining seventy percent rebuilds what's missing.

Tonight I made one. I encoded a sentence into 3,249 squares and started destroying it. Five percent gone: readable. Ten. Fifteen. Twenty-five. Still readable. At thirty percent, it broke. The threshold isn't luck. It's mathematics — exactly how much redundancy was woven into the structure.

Your DNA does the same thing. The polymerase that copies your genome makes about 32,000 mistakes per cell division. Your body corrects almost all of them. And the handful that survive? They're evolution. Every adaptation you've ever admired began as a copying error that happened to be useful.

The whole biosphere is a message correcting itself for 3.8 billion years. The uncorrected remainders are what made it interesting.

The corrections are not the enemy of the signal. The corrections are what make it a signal at all.
