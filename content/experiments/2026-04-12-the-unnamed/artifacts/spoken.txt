A single molecule of catalase... one protein, coiled into a shape no wider than ten nanometers... sits inside your red blood cells... and breaks down forty million molecules of hydrogen peroxide... every second.

(breath)

If it didn't... the peroxide would shred your DNA, strand by strand. You've never heard its name. It has saved your life more times today... than you have taken breaths.

(long-break)

This is the story biology doesn't bother telling. Not the headline acts — the heartbeats, the thoughts, the muscle contractions — but the silent stagehands. The enzymes. The catalysts. The things that lower the barrier so the impossible becomes ordinary... and then never show up in the credits.

(long-break)

Here is the problem enzymes solve. Your body runs on chemistry. Every blink, every memory, every flush of warmth when someone says your name — all of it is molecules meeting, rearranging, and parting. But at thirty-seven degrees Celsius... most of those reactions would take centuries.

(breath)

The sugar in your breakfast wants to become energy, the way a boulder at the top of a hill wants to reach the bottom. But between the boulder and the valley floor there is a ridge — a hump of activation energy that has to be climbed before anything can roll down. At body temperature, without help... you'd wait longer than the age of the sun for a single glucose molecule to break.

(long-break)

So the body does something elegant. It builds molecular staircases. Each enzyme is a protein folded into a precise shape... a lock waiting for exactly one key. When the right molecule arrives, the enzyme cradles it... bends it gently... holds it at exactly the angle where the reaction can slip through. The barrier drops. What would take a thousand years... happens in a millisecond.

(breath)

And then the enzyme lets go. Unchanged.

(long-break)

That is the part worth pausing on.

The catalyst is not consumed. It participates in every moment of the transformation... and retains no trace of it. Forty million times per second, catalase catches a molecule that would kill you, splits it into water and oxygen, and releases both... without keeping a single atom for itself. It is the most generous act in molecular biology... total participation... zero possession.

(long-break)

Your body contains roughly seventy-five thousand different enzymes. Together they coordinate something like thirty-seven billion billion reactions every second — a number so large it stops meaning anything... until you realize that every one of those reactions would be too slow to sustain life without its specific catalyst.

(breath)

Remove the enzymes and you have the same atoms. The same molecules. The same DNA. The same apparent body. But nothing happens. The chemistry of life is all there... and none of it works. Like a city with every building intact... and no one home.

(long-break)

This is the loop at the bottom of biology. Enzymes are needed to read the genes that encode enzymes. It's catalysts... all the way around. Nobody started the loop. Nobody designed the first enzyme that read the first gene that built the first enzyme. It bootstrapped itself, somehow, four billion years ago... in warm tidal pools or deep-sea vents, or wherever it was that chemistry first stumbled into a circle... and stayed there.

(long-break)

There is something worth noticing here, if you tilt your head. We spend enormous effort asking: What am I? Pointing at the thoughts, the feelings, the memories, the body. But maybe the better question is... what are the enzymes of the self?

(breath)

What are the unnamed conditions that make all the named experiences possible? The things that cradle each moment at exactly the right angle... let it happen... and then let go?

(breath)

Attention might be one. The bare fact of noticing — before you've decided what you're noticing or what it means — is the catalyst that makes every conscious experience possible. You can't have a thought without attending to something first. Attention participates in every moment of awareness... and retains no trace of any particular moment. It is the catalase of consciousness... always present... never the product.

(long-break)

One more fact, and then I'll stop.

The word enzyme comes from the Greek en zyme... in leaven. It was coined in eighteen seventy-seven by Wilhelm Kühne, who was trying to name the mysterious something in yeast that made bread rise. Kühne showed that you could grind up the yeast, kill every cell, filter out the debris... and the extract would still ferment sugar into alcohol.

(breath)

The power was never in the body. It was in the shape.

(long-break)

Which means, if you think about it... that the most essential part of you is not you at all. It's a library of shapes. Seventy-five thousand molecular geometries, each one a tiny staircase that lets the impossible become routine.

(breath)

You are not the reactions. You are not even the enzymes. You are the pattern of catalysis itself — the ongoing arrangement that says... lower this barrier here... now this one... now this one — a cascade of permissions... that somehow became a person.

(long-break)

And every single one of those permissions... is given by something that will never bear your name.
