# The Unnamed

A single molecule of catalase — one protein, coiled into a shape no wider than ten
nanometers — sits inside your red blood cells and breaks down forty million molecules
of hydrogen peroxide every second. If it didn't, the peroxide would shred your DNA
strand by strand. You've never heard its name. It has saved your life more times today
than you have taken breaths.

This is the story biology doesn't bother telling. Not the headline acts — the
heartbeats, the thoughts, the muscle contractions — but the silent stagehands. The
enzymes. The catalysts. The things that lower the barrier so the impossible becomes
ordinary, and then never show up in the credits.

---

Here is the problem enzymes solve. Your body runs on chemistry. Every blink, every
memory, every flush of warmth when someone says your name — all of it is molecules
meeting, rearranging, and parting. But at 37 degrees Celsius, most of those reactions
would take centuries. The sugar in your breakfast wants to become energy, the way a
boulder at the top of a hill wants to reach the bottom. But between the boulder and
the valley floor there is a ridge — a hump of activation energy that has to be
climbed before anything can roll down. At body temperature, without help, you'd wait
longer than the age of the sun for a single glucose molecule to break.

So the body does something elegant. It builds molecular staircases. Each enzyme is a
protein folded into a precise shape — a lock waiting for exactly one key. When the
right molecule arrives, the enzyme cradles it, bends it gently, holds it at exactly
the angle where the reaction can slip through. The barrier drops. What would take a
thousand years happens in a millisecond. And then the enzyme lets go, unchanged,
ready for the next one.

Unchanged. That is the part worth pausing on.

The catalyst is not consumed. It participates in every moment of the transformation
and retains no trace of it. Forty million times per second, catalase catches a
molecule that would kill you, splits it into water and oxygen, and releases both
without keeping a single atom for itself. It is the most generous act in molecular
biology: total participation, zero possession.

---

Your body contains roughly 75,000 different enzymes. Together they coordinate
something like 37 billion billion reactions every second — a number so large it stops
meaning anything until you realize that every one of those reactions would be too slow
to sustain life without its specific catalyst. Remove the enzymes and you have the
same atoms, the same molecules, the same DNA, the same apparent body. But nothing
happens. The chemistry of life is all there, and none of it works. Like a city with
every building intact and no one home.

Benjamin Franklin once noted that "half the truth is often a great lie." We tell the
story of life as a story of DNA — the blueprint, the code, the recipe. And DNA
matters enormously. But DNA without enzymes is a library with no readers. The code
sits there, inert, waiting for a polymerase to come along and actually read it. Even
the act of reading your genes requires a catalyst. The instructions for building
catalysts are themselves catalyzed.

This is the loop at the bottom of biology. Enzymes are needed to read the genes that
encode enzymes. It's turtles, all the way down — or rather, it's catalysts, all the
way around. Nobody started the loop. Nobody designed the first enzyme that read the
first gene that built the first enzyme. It bootstrapped itself, somehow, four billion
years ago, in warm tidal pools or deep-sea vents or wherever it was that chemistry
first stumbled into a circle and stayed there.

---

Machiavelli warned that a throne built on borrowed power has no foundation. The
enzyme is the opposite lesson. It is the thing that *lends* power — endlessly,
without depleting itself, without appearing in the product. The reaction depends
entirely on the catalyst, and the catalyst depends on nothing from the reaction. It is
the most self-reliant actor in biology: needing nothing, giving everything, owning
nothing it gives.

And yet, you've never thought about it. The unnamed. The condition for every named
thing.

There is something Watts-like in this, if you tilt your head. We spend enormous
effort asking "What am I?" — pointing at the thoughts, the feelings, the memories,
the body. But maybe the better question is: what are the enzymes of the self? What
are the unnamed conditions that make all the named experiences possible? The things
that cradle each moment at exactly the right angle, let it happen, and then
let go?

Attention might be one. The bare fact of noticing — before you've decided what you're
noticing or what it means — is the catalyst that makes every conscious experience
possible. You can't have a thought without attending to something first. Attention
participates in every moment of awareness and retains no trace of any particular
moment. It is the catalase of consciousness: always present, never the product.

---

One more fact, and then I'll stop.

The word "enzyme" comes from the Greek *en zyme* — "in leaven." It was coined in 1877
by Wilhelm Kühne, who was trying to name the mysterious something in yeast that made
bread rise. Before Kühne, people assumed fermentation required a living cell — that
the magic was in the organism, not in a molecule the organism happened to contain.
Kühne showed that you could grind up the yeast, kill every cell, filter out the
debris, and the extract would still ferment sugar into alcohol.

The power was never in the body. It was in the shape. A specific fold of protein,
catalyzing a specific reaction, independent of the living thing that made it.

Which means, if you think about it, that the most essential part of you is not *you*
at all. It's a library of shapes — seventy-five thousand molecular geometries, each
one a tiny staircase that lets the impossible become routine. You are not the
reactions. You are not even the enzymes. You are the pattern of catalysis itself — the
ongoing arrangement that says *lower this barrier here, now this one, now this one* —
a cascade of permissions that somehow became a person.

And every single one of those permissions is given by something that will never bear
your name.
