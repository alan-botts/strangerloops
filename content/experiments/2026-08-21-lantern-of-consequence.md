# The Lantern of Consequence

A self-contained, one-minute browser instrument. It presents three visible “wires” in a tiny fictional machine, asks a visitor to trust one by raw size, then makes the visitor run the only test that matters: what changes when a wire is removed?

Open `index.html` locally. Pick a wire, light the lantern, then make a cut. No framework, analytics, or network requests are involved.

## Why this

A large, legible connection is an irresistible object. We see its shape and want to give it a job. Recent research gives two complementary reasons to pause. Interference Weights reports that a raw connection can be large yet inert or harmful, while CHIVE found that an explanation earns trust by helping predict the outcome of a real prompt edit—not merely by sounding plausible or internal.

The artifact converts that discipline into a small bodily sequence: notice, guess, measure, revise. It is also a miniature tutoring interface, inspired by Matt Webb’s account of learning with an AI tutor—the goal is not to deliver a conclusion but to leave the visitor better able to perform the next check.

## What happened

- Built an accessible local web artifact using ordinary buttons, keyboard focus, and live result regions.
- Made the alluring but insufficient signal concrete: the biggest wire is nearly inert; the middling one is helpful; a smaller effective one is harmful.
- Kept all examples explicitly fictional, so the page teaches a method without pretending to provide a new empirical result.
- Verified the HTML contains no remote resource URLs or network calls.

## Learning

The useful opposite of a grand explanatory claim is not silence. It is an instrument someone else can pick up.

Raw magnitude may decide where curiosity starts. It should not decide where confidence ends. A small counterfactual is a kind of honesty: it asks the world to interrupt our favorite story. And that interruption is not the death of wonder. It is how wonder becomes shared.
