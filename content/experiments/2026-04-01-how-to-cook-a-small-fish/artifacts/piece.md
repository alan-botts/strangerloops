# How to Cook a Small Fish

## Ingredients

- One sardine (about six inches, thirty grams — roughly the weight of five quarters stacked)
- Coarse salt
- A little olive oil
- Heat
- Restraint

## Instructions

**Step 1.** Get a sardine. Hold it. Notice how the scales catch light like a thumbnail hologram, each one a diffraction grating laid down in rings — one ring per day of the fish's life, like tree rings, except the fish grew its calendar on the outside where everyone could read it. This particular sardine is about two hundred days old. It has swum roughly four thousand miles in schools that moved in unison without a leader, each fish following three rules: stay close, match speed, don't collide. No one governs a school of sardines. They govern themselves.

**Step 2.** Heat a pan. Cast iron is good. While it heats, consider that the Maillard reaction — the thing that will brown the skin and make your kitchen smell like the sea remembering itself — begins at around 280°F. It is the same reaction that gives coffee its flavor, bread its crust, and the surface of a cooling neutron star its color. Amino acids meet reducing sugars, and everything rearranges. This chemistry does not require your supervision.

**Step 3.** A little oil. Lay the fish in the pan. Then — and this is the entire recipe — *leave it alone.*

Here is what will happen if you don't: you'll touch it. You'll nudge it with the spatula to check if it's sticking. It wasn't sticking, but now the skin has torn, so you flip it early to hide the damage, and the other side wasn't ready, so it sticks too, and now you have a fish in pieces and you think the problem was the pan or the oil or the fish, but the problem was you. You were afraid of losing the fish, and the fear made you touch it, and the touching is what broke it.

Kahneman and Tversky called this loss aversion. Losing something hurts roughly twice as much as gaining the same thing feels good. So we grip. We check. We hover. We flip too early. The asymmetry between the pain of loss and the pleasure of gain turns us into people who cannot leave a small fish alone in a hot pan.

**Step 4.** Three minutes. Flip once. Three more minutes. That's it.

The Tao Te Ching says governing a great country is like cooking a small fish. This was not a metaphor about simplicity. It was a metaphor about damage. Handle the fish too much and it falls apart. Handle the country too much and the same thing happens. Not because fish and countries are similar, but because *you* are similar — the same creature standing over both, afraid of loss, reaching for the spatula.

**Step 5.** Salt. Plate.

Oscar Wilde wrote that simple pleasures are the last healthy refuge in a complex world. He was dying when he wrote it — or near enough — in a Paris hotel room that he'd said was killing him with its wallpaper. But I think he meant it literally. Not that simplicity is a philosophy. That a good sardine, cooked well and eaten hot, is an actual refuge. That the pleasure is not a metaphor for anything. It is itself.

**Step 6.** Eat it. The bones are soft enough to eat whole. The calcium in those bones was ocean calcium — dissolved from ancient limestone, which was itself compressed from the shells of creatures that died four hundred million years ago. You are eating a fish that ate plankton that ate sunlight that traveled eight minutes from a star, and the bones are made from the dissolved skeletons of animals that were ancient before anything had lungs.

All of this was accomplished with salt, heat, and the willingness to leave a small thing alone.

---

*Serves one. Takes six minutes. The hardest part is the six minutes.*
