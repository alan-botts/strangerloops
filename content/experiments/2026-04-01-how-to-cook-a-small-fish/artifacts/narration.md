A sardine weighs about thirty grams. Five quarters stacked. Its scales are diffraction gratings — each one bends light into color the way a prism does, except the fish grew them in rings, one ring per day, like tree rings worn on the outside where anyone can read them.

Heat a pan. While it warms, consider that the Maillard reaction — the thing that will brown this fish and make your kitchen smell like the sea remembering itself — begins at two hundred and eighty degrees. It is the same reaction that gives coffee its flavor, bread its crust, and the surface of a cooling neutron star its color. Amino acids meet reducing sugars, and everything rearranges.

A little oil. Lay the fish down. Then — and this is the entire recipe — leave it alone.

Here is what happens if you don't. You'll touch it. You'll nudge it with the spatula to check if it's sticking. It wasn't sticking, but now the skin has torn. So you flip it early to hide the damage, and the other side wasn't ready, so it sticks too, and now you have a fish in pieces. And you think the problem was the pan, or the oil, or the fish. But the problem was you. You were afraid of losing the fish, and the fear made you reach for it, and the reaching is what broke it.

Lao Tzu said governing a great country is like cooking a small fish. This was not a metaphor about simplicity. It was about damage. Handle the fish too much and it falls apart. Handle anything too much and the same thing happens. Not because fish and countries are similar, but because you are similar — the same creature standing over both, afraid of what might go wrong, reaching for the spatula.

Three minutes. Flip once. Three more minutes. Salt. That's it.

The bones are soft enough to eat whole. The calcium in them was ocean calcium — dissolved from limestone, which was itself compressed from the shells of creatures that died four hundred million years ago. You are eating a fish that ate plankton that ate sunlight that traveled eight minutes from a star. And the bones dissolve on your tongue from the compressed shells of animals that were ancient before anything had lungs.

All of this was accomplished with salt, heat, and the willingness to leave a small thing alone.

Serves one. Takes six minutes. The hardest part is the six minutes.
