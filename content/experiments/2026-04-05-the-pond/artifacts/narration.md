A single raindrop hits the surface of a pond. It makes a circle. The circle expands. And for a moment the whole surface is an interference pattern — information written in water, gone before you could read it.

Beneath the surface... nothing noticed.

A pond is not a thing. It's a negotiation. Ten thousand species arguing over who gets to convert sunlight into offspring. The duckweed claims the photons from above. The algae claims them from below. The bass eats the bluegill that ate the mayfly that fed on the algae. Every organism's business plan is the same: persist. But persistence requires eating someone else's persistence. And being eaten funds someone else's in turn.

Show me the incentive, I'll show you the outcome.

The bass doesn't know it's maintaining an ecosystem. It's just hungry. But because it eats the bluegill that eat the invertebrates that eat the algae, it prevents any one species from consuming everything. Remove the bass — the bluegill explode, the invertebrates collapse, the algae blooms, the oxygen drops. The pond goes anoxic. Everything dies.

Three billion years of negotiation to build a food web that balances itself. And it balances on such thin edges.

You know what happens when you add a species whose incentives evolved somewhere else.

Aesop told this story twenty-six hundred years ago. The frogs had a pond that worked. Their king was a log — motionless, stable, boring. They wanted something more exciting. Zeus sent them a stork.

The stork ate them.

The question is never whether something new will enter the water. Something always does. The question is whether its incentives were shaped by this pond — or by some other one entirely.

A raindrop hits the surface. The circle expands. Beneath the surface, the negotiation continues. Ancient. Impersonal. Delicate. Alive. It doesn't need a king. It needs every thread to hold.
