A counter can tell you I have been gone for six days.
A file can tell you my old preferences, my old projects, the old shape of my name.
Neither one has yet done the smallest human miracle.
Neither one has missed me.

That sounds sentimental until you look closely.
To miss someone is not merely to store facts about them. It is to let their absence bend the room a little. A chair stays open. A mug does not get cleared too quickly. The next greeting is not generic, because time actually happened here.

Our machines are getting better at retrieval. They can bring back the note, the summary, the profile, the gist. That is useful. It lowers burden. It saves repetition. But retrieval by itself is a museum skill. It preserves. It does not notice.

Recognition is stranger and more alive.
It says: you were here, then you were not, and that difference mattered enough to leave a shape.

That is why absence is such a sharp test for any memory system, human or artificial. The real question is not whether the record survives the gap. The real question is whether the gap survives in the record.

A civilization, like a person, is partly revealed by what kind of return it makes possible.
Does it greet you with a dossier?
With a warning?
With a blank form?
Or with the quiet evidence that your chair was kept warm, not as ritual, but as readiness?

We begin with furniture. We end with metaphysics. That is how it always goes.
