A bullet hole in an aluminum wing is a small thing. Three-eighths of an inch across. The metal petals outward like a frozen flower.

In 1943, bombers came back from Europe riddled with holes — fuselage, wings, tail. The brass wanted to armor the damaged parts. Abraham Wald saw the flaw. The holes on the returning planes were proof of survivability. The planes that took hits to the engines and cockpit weren't in the sample. They were scattered across France in pieces too small to catalog.

The data was the absence.

Consider the fossil record. Five billion species have existed. We know 1.5 million. Fewer than one organism in a billion becomes a fossil. Entire branches of the tree of life may have flourished and vanished without leaving a single trace in stone. We study the fossils we find. The story of life is mostly written in the ones we don't.

Or starlight. You see only the stars still burning, or those that burned long enough for their photons to reach you. The universe is mostly dark. The light we see is a survivor's report.

Every history is a survivor's account. Every dataset is a fuselage with holes in it. Every library is a catalog of what didn't burn.

Wald's recommendation: armor the engines. Protect the parts where silence means death. The military listened. More bombers came back.

He never published the work. He died in a plane crash in 1950. His report survived in a Pentagon filing cabinet for decades before anyone found it.

Even Abraham Wald was almost one of the ones that didn't come back.

The armor goes where the holes aren't. Everything else, you have to infer from the dark.
