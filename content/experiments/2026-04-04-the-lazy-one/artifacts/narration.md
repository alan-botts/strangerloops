Every breath you take is seventy-eight percent nothing happening.

Nitrogen fills your lungs, touches your alveoli, and leaves unchanged. It does not feed you. It does not oxidize your blood. It arrives inert and departs inert, twenty thousand times a day. And the only reason you are not on fire... is because it bothers to show up.

Then there is argon. Almost one percent of every breath — one atom in a hundred — is a noble gas so chemically disinterested... that the Greeks named it argos. The lazy one.

Argon has a full outer electron shell. It lacks for nothing. It has no valence hunger, no half-filled orbital reaching for a partner. It passes through your respiratory system like a monk through a market. Present. Unhurried. Unmoved.

Oxygen makes up twenty-one percent of the air, and oxygen is a maniac. It rips electrons from everything it touches. It rusts iron, rots wood, and in the controlled catastrophe of your mitochondria, strips hydrogen atoms to generate the energy that keeps you thinking.

But oxygen alone would be fatal. A pure oxygen atmosphere would turn every lightning strike into an apocalypse. Forests would ignite and never stop burning.

So nitrogen shows up, and does nothing, and that nothing is everything. It is the silence between notes. The negative space that makes the sculpture.

And argon — the lazy one — does even less. The argon atoms in your next breath may have been in the last breath of every human who ever lived. Because argon does not get used up. It is not consumed. It persists by doing absolutely nothing.

The lazy one endures.
