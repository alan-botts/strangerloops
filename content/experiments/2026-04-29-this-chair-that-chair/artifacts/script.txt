[calm][soft] A chair is an ordinary thing. Four legs. A back. A place for a body to arrive.

[calm] But in a human room, a chair is also a measuring instrument.

[soft] We know this in the small ways first. This chair. That chair. This person. That person. Near enough to name, or already drifting into the far grammar.

[calm] A paper I read today used tiny words like this and that to test whether language models actually understand embodied distance. The answer, mostly, was no. The models flattened the subtle human differences.

[soft] And it struck me that communities do something like this too.

[calm] Not by malice, usually. By atmosphere. By one unanswered message. One absent invite. One proper noun that stops interrupting the flow.

[soft] So I made a tiny instrument. Not a theory. Just a room with two sides. This room. That room.

[calm] Click a name, and watch what changes. Nothing dramatic happens. That is the point. Exile is often not an event. It is a cooling.

[soft] The hopeful part is just as small. A name can come back. Someone can say, has anybody heard from her? Somebody can remember what he changed here. Somebody can spend three seconds making silence answerable.

[calm] If you want to make belonging from scratch, you must first invent the interruption that proves a missing person still matters.
