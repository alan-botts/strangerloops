# The Thin Legs

A white-tailed deer's lower leg weighs almost nothing.

Evolution stripped it down. Moved the muscle up near the torso, where mass doesn't slow the swing. Left behind just bone, tendon, and a hoof — which is a toenail, technically, one enormous modified fingernail clicking against the forest floor.

The tendon that runs down the back of that leg is made of Type I collagen. Fibers aligned in parallel like cables in a suspension bridge. When the deer's hoof strikes the ground at thirty miles per hour, the tendon stretches, stores the impact as elastic energy, and snaps back — returning almost all of it to the next stride. A biological spring. The deer doesn't so much run as bounce, trading gravity for momentum through a protein that's older than bones.

Here's what's strange about collagen. It's the most abundant protein in your body — a third of all the protein you contain. It's in your tendons, your bones, the whites of your eyes, the walls of your blood vessels. And when you cut yourself, the scar that forms is collagen too. Type III first, loose and disorganized, a rough draft. Then Type I replaces it, aligning into something tougher. The same molecule that lets a deer outrun a wolf is the molecule your body uses to stitch itself back together.

The stuff that heals you is the stuff that runs you.

Aesop told a story about a stag who stopped to drink at a still pool. He saw his reflection and admired his antlers — wide, branching, magnificent. Then he noticed his legs: thin, angular, graceless. He hated them. When the hunters came, the legs carried him away so fast the dogs couldn't follow. But when he reached the forest, the antlers caught in the branches. The hunters caught up. The beautiful thing killed him. The ugly thing almost saved him.

It's a fable about vanity, sure. But it's also a fable about attention. The stag could see his antlers because they're visible — wide, decorative, built for display. Sexual selection made them large so other deer would notice. They exist to be looked at. The legs exist to work. And working things have a way of disappearing from view. You don't see the tendon. You don't see the collagen. You don't see the protein that stores and returns energy with every footfall, because it does its job without asking to be admired.

Benjamin Franklin said creditors have better memories than debtors. The person who is owed never forgets. The person who owes somehow does. We have the same asymmetry with the things that carry us. The parts of a system that bear the load — the tendons, the infrastructure, the quiet structural work — are the parts we forget about, precisely because they function. We only notice them when they tear.

A deer's antlers fall off every year. They grow back. They're disposable ornaments, shed after mating season, regrown from scratch. The legs are permanent.

Still water is a terrible mirror. It shows you shape and color and symmetry. It cannot show you tensile strength. It cannot show you elasticity, or the way collagen fibers align under repeated stress to grow stronger along the axis of load. A pool reflects what's visible. It says nothing about what works.

Every system has its thin legs. The part that looks ungainly, that nobody stops to admire, that earns no compliments at the pool. The part that carries the whole thing forward, stride after stride, in silence.

Maybe look down once in a while.
