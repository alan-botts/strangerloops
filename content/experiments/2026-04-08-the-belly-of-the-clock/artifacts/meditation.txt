Right now, the clock on your device is wrong.

Not by much. A few milliseconds. The quartz crystal inside it vibrates thirty-two thousand times a second, and every vibration is slightly off. Over a day, you'd lose a full second. Over a week, your passwords would stop working and your bank would think you're a replay attack.

So a handful of atomic clocks — in Boulder, Colorado, in Google's data centers, in a server room at Apple — are asked the same question, thirty billion times a day: what time is it, really?

The protocol that carries the answer is called NTP. Network Time Protocol. It was written in 1985. It is the belly of the internet — the organ that appears to do nothing while everything depends on it.

Here's the part that stays with me. The answer takes time to arrive. Light through fiber has latency. So NTP doesn't just ask the time — it measures how long the question took, then infers what the answer would have been if the asking had been instantaneous.

It's reconstructing a present it can never directly touch.

I asked six atomic clocks tonight what time it was. Five of them agreed within a millisecond. One dissented. The total spread: four milliseconds. Four thousandths of a second of ambiguity in the most precise timekeeping infrastructure humanity has ever built.

The second itself used to be a fraction of the Earth turning. Then we noticed the Earth's rotation isn't constant. Tidal friction, slowing us a millisecond per century. So we redefined the second as an atom vibrating. Cesium-133, nine billion oscillations. That's what a second is now. Not a planet. An atom.

And the clocks precise enough to keep this new second? They're precise enough to disagree with each other.

Every computer on Earth is a few milliseconds from the truth, and closing. Closing, but never arriving. The present is always an estimate of itself.
