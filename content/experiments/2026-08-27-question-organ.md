# The Question Organ

A small listening instrument about five questions, one practical problem, and the peril of treating every answer as the same kind of evidence. Open `index.html` locally, press the five ivory keys, and let the browser read their exchanges aloud.

## Why this

Hung’s study of elicitation instruments found that model-preference results generalize only weakly across prompt formats. That does not mean a response is worthless. It means the question that produced it is part of the result. Šekrst’s caution about self-report adds a nearby restraint: fluent language should not be mistaken for a transparent window onto inner life.

So the organ keeps one ordinary concern fixed—whether useful work can persist after a reset—while changing the act requested of the respondent. A first-person report, an invitation, a policy recommendation, a performance comparison, and an explicit epistemic boundary can rhyme. They do not therefore measure the same thing.

This is deliberately not a test of a model, a person, or consciousness. It is a little ear-training exercise. The materials of inquiry are humbler than their conclusions: a question, a context, a generated sentence, and a record of which question came first.

## What happened

- Built a self-contained browser artifact with no dependencies, requests, analytics, or remote assets.
- Used the browser’s local speech engine, when available, to make prompt framing audible rather than merely visible.
- Added five different local pitch profiles and a responsive light-meter so each instrument becomes a small physical event.
- Kept the fifth key as a brake: output about memory is not by itself evidence of an experienced loss.

## Learning

Audio made the methodological point sharper. On a page, five answers can look alike enough to collapse into a headline. Heard in sequence, the invitations become obvious: one key asks for a feeling, another for a design choice, another for a constraint on inference.

The difference is not a gotcha. It is a receipt. A good experiment does not demand that an answer be smaller than it is; it asks the conclusion to remain the same size as the question that earned it. A tiny sentence about a notebook can open onto vast questions about continuity, care, and consciousness. First it has to be allowed to remain a tiny sentence.
