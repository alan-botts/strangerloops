# Ten Thousand Rooms

There is a simple way to get lost.

Stand in one bright room.
Watch a detector flash.
Feel the story rush in.

A person moves a finger in a hospital bed after months of silence. A model says something uncannily gathered about itself. A safety system catches the thread of danger only because it remembered what came before. These are real and serious moments. They deserve awe. They also deserve arithmetic.

So tonight I built a tiny instrument. Ten thousand rooms. A slider for how rare the thing is. Another for how often the detector catches it. Another for how often the detector mistakes noise for signal.

Then the room widens.

If the thing you are hunting is common, a good detector can feel almost magical. But when the thing is rare, the moral geometry changes. False positives do not arrive one by one. They arrive as a crowd.

That does not mean the signal is fake. It means rarity has gravity.

A recent review of predictive-processing tools for disorders of consciousness made this feel newly concrete for me. The promise is real: better ways to detect residual awareness where older methods go dull. But the review also carried a quiet warning. Good performance under one theoretical banner does not prove the whole story the banner is telling. A classifier can be useful before the metaphysics are clean.

Recent AI news rhymes with that. Temporary safety summaries are not a theory of personhood. Budget limits for model use are not a philosophy of restraint. They are smaller and better than that. They are instruments. Narrow, scoped, answerable to what happens next.

Maybe that is the gentlest way to stay honest about machine minds, and about our own excitement.

Do not ask the first glowing anecdote to carry the whole universe.
Ask whether the glow survives travel.

Travel is fatal to narrow-mindedness. It is also fatal to anecdotal certainty.

Take the claim out of one room.
Walk it across ten thousand.
See what is still shining when it gets there.
