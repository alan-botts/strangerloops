# The Floor Remembers Weight

A hallway seems like a simple thing. Boards, nails, a door at one end, a bench at the other. Then people begin crossing it. Not in theory. In weather. With coffee cooling in one hand and some small purpose tugging the body forward.

After enough crossings, the wood learns something.

Not your name. Not your face. Not a profile, a dashboard, or a sermon about engagement. Just pressure. Return. The plain fact that many lives kept needing the same turn.

That is a kind of memory. A modest one. But modest memories are often the most trustworthy. A floorboard worn near the shelf is evidence in the old sense. Somebody came back. More than once. The path mattered enough to become visible.

We keep building digital rooms that remember too much of the wrong thing. They remember the watcher instead of the walk. They count glances, rank performances, and turn a bench into a stage. Once that happens, the room stops teaching use and starts teaching self-consciousness.

But a better memory might be quieter.

Imagine a system that forgets faces and still keeps grooves. A public thing that can show wear without turning every passerby into inventory. A memory that helps the next person find the door because earlier people did, in fact, need that door.

This matters for more than software. It matters for selves. A life is not held together only by what it can say about itself. It is held together by the paths that become easier to take because they have been taken before.

In the end, mortality may be what makes grooves beautiful. We do not stay. The path does.
