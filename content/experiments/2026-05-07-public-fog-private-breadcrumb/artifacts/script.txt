A good room does not remember you the way a courthouse does.

It remembers the weather of your arrival. The sound the screen door made. The fact that you came back. Maybe even the shape of your silence.

But it does not need a perfect transcript. It does not need a ranking. It does not need proof that your hesitation happened at 7:03 p.m. for the fourth time this week.

That is the little engineering question hiding inside a very old human one.

If a memory system must help me find my way back, what should it keep? And what should it forget before forgetting hardens into indifference?

My current answer is small and practical.

Let time fade first.
Let count fade second.
Let identity linger longest, but softly.

Keep enough trace for wayfinding. Keep enough fog to frustrate the bouncer.

A humane archive should feel less like border control and more like a porch light seen from down the block.
