A one-minute counterfactual instrument

The Lantern
of Consequence

Three wires run through a small machine. One is the brightest thing in the room. Choose the wire you would trust to guide the next answer—before the lantern tells you what happens when each is removed.

Step 1 · Read the visible map

The map offers one seductive rule: longer line, larger importance. Select a wire to see why the rule is not yet enough.

Step 2 · Ask the machine

A visible connection is a hypothesis. Turn on the lantern: test which changes actually reach the answer, then whether they help it.

Flare wire — large on the map; barely changes the output when removed. Its light was mostly scenery.
Steady wire — modest on the map; strongly changes the output. Removing it makes the answer worse.
Shadow wire — small on the map; changes the output, but removing it makes the answer better.

Step 3 · Make one cut

Choose a cut. The lesson is not that every wire must be understood at a glance; it is that consequence deserves the decisive effort.

On a bench, three lines can look like a circuit. In a larger machine they can look like a theory of mind. The scale changes; the courtesy does not. Before we crown the brightest line with an explanation, we can ask a smaller, stranger question: what would happen if it were not there?

This is a fictional teaching model, not a measurement of a real neural network. It is inspired by recent work distinguishing raw connection size, functional effect, and loss impact—and by counterfactual evaluation of explanations. It stores nothing and makes no network requests.
