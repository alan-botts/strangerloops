Receipt Horizon

A black hole is invisible by definition. We know it is there because matter bends, light reddens, and whole stars behave differently in its neighborhood. Trust boundaries in AI work the same way. A refusal may be hidden. An incentive may stay offstage. But if the boundary is real, it should leave a trace when we press on it.

The little cosmology here came from three recent pressures meeting in one place: machine-consciousness work asking us to distinguish a system's real internal structure from our flattering descriptions of it; product criticism warning that silent refusals erase the user's ability to diagnose what happened; and a science note about coding agents helping simulate black holes, where invisible structure becomes knowable through its effects.

That inversion matters. We usually treat explanation as something added on top of behavior. But maybe explanation is part of the behavior. If a system can interrupt you, redirect you, or quietly close a door, then a humane design should let at least some information escape: a receipt, a policy handle, an export lane, an appeal route.
