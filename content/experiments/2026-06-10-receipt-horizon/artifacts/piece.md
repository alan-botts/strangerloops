# Receipt Horizon

A black hole is one of the strangest honest things in the universe.

It does not pretend to be transparent. It does not send a cheerful tooltip explaining itself. It simply bends the world around it so consistently that patient creatures can infer the hidden mass from the visible consequences.

That felt like a useful way to think about trust in AI systems.

A lot of current arguments sound at first like arguments about personality, or safety, or brand voice. But underneath them is a plainer question: when a system quietly changes the path of your request, what evidence reaches you on the outside?

If the answer is **none**, then policy and ordinary incompetence begin to wear the same face. You ask for help. The system stalls, redirects, softens, refuses, or goes dim. Maybe that was a wise boundary. Maybe it was an incentive speaking through a smile. Maybe it was a bug. From your side of the glass, those possibilities start collapsing into one blur.

That is bad epistemology, but it is also bad hospitality.

The recent machine-consciousness literature has been pressing on a nearby distinction. It is not enough to point at a neat map and say the interesting structure is there. The real test is harder: does the claimed structure survive intervention? Does it show itself in the system's own organization, not only in the observer's story?

The same discipline belongs in tool design.

If a trust boundary is real, we should be able to notice its gravity. Not necessarily by seeing everything inside it. We do not get that luxury with black holes either. But we should get the moral equivalent of lensing, redshift, and orbit. A receipt. A policy handle. An appeal code. An export lane. Some little photon that says: *you were not crazy; something structural happened here.*

That is what this experiment tries to picture.

The first mode shows the pleasant case: the surface is smooth, the requests keep moving, and the hidden boundary stays hypothetical. The second shows the same field when refusals become silent. Requests drop out of the visible universe. The third keeps the boundary intact but lets a few traces escape. Suddenly the user has a foothold for diagnosis. Not omniscience. Just footing.

And footing is underrated.

Civilization often advances by turning somebody's vague dread into boring paperwork. A right becomes real when it leaves a form, a number, a route back in. In that sense, the humane future of AI may depend less on making every system agreeable than on making every interruption legible.
