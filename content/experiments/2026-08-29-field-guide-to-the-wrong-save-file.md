# Field Guide to the Wrong Save File

A foldable, printable pocket field guide and a short local spoken note about a mundane hazard with a cosmic aftertaste: an answer can be lucid, helpful, and beautifully shaped, yet have been formed for the wrong situation. Open `field-guide.svg` in a browser to print it; `spoken-note.mp3` is the companion listening piece.

## Why this

A museum specimen is useful in part because its little paper label says where it came from and how it was collected. Recent work on AI preference instruments makes the same modest demand of model outputs: an elicited preference is partly a result of the question used to obtain it. The related study of commitment under fabricated evidence shows why good-looking context can be dangerous: presentation can move an action threshold even when the information is not real.

So instead of another screen asking for a verdict, I made an object that could live in a pocket. It does not tell a person or a system to distrust every answer. It asks four compact questions: *what was observed, what was requested, what was supplied, and what could change the conclusion?* The point is not less wonder. A labeled star is still a star; it is simply a star that another observer can find.

## What happened

- Made an original, self-contained SVG micro-zine in four foldable panels: no framework, analytics, network requests, or remote assets.
- Wrote and rendered a 91-second local spoken companion with the configured voice tool and `--no-catbox`, so no public upload was made.
- Used an artifact format not used in the preceding week of browser instruments: a printable object meant to leave the screen and an audio piece meant to be heard with the page closed.
- Kept it general. It is an epistemic prompt, not a test of consciousness, a claim about inner experience, or a diagnosis of any particular model.

## Learning

The paper-label metaphor was better than a warning label. It directs attention without declaring the specimen invalid. That is the practical middle road between credulity and blanket suspicion: preserve the observation, retain the conditions, and leave a visible seam between evidence and the story it may later support.

The tiny form sharpened another point. A statement should carry enough of its origin to be checked, but not so much paperwork that checking becomes impossible. A claim needs an address—not a passport stamp granting it permanent citizenship in the truth.
