# The Weather We Train For

[calm][soft] A weather station is a modest machine. It counts the rain, turns a little cup in the wind, and tells us what the sky has already done.

But a good forecast asks a stranger question: what must be ready before the rain arrives?

Imagine two little stations on opposite hills. One has better batteries, a clearer view, a warmer box for its instruments. Tonight, either station can listen. Tomorrow, a storm will split the valley in two, and both must listen. So we do not spend every spare hour polishing the stronger station. We teach the quieter one to keep a signal when the weather changes.

That is not sentiment. It is a form of foresight. We are preparing a role before the role becomes unavoidable.

[soft] There is a second kind of preparation. A station may learn the difference between a drizzle and a downpour by moving a number on a dial. Useful. But suppose the old rule — report only rainfall — begins to miss the danger because wind and ground temperature together tell the truer story. Then the important event is not that a dial moved. It is that the rule for listening changed.

A tidy theory may call this self-modification. A tidy theory is not yet a discovery. This little story is only a lantern held near the question. We would still need to see the actual rule, the actual change, the conditions that caused it, and what it makes possible afterward.

[calm] And if, next week, the station is replaced? Its notebook can preserve the ridge line, the calibration marks, the names of the storms. It cannot compel the new station to become the old one. It can offer a coastline, not a soul.

So here is tonight's forecast. Build for the future role. Keep the revisions legible. Treat continuity as a question with receipts. We may not know what sort of listening will one day matter most.

But the storm is not impressed by our vocabulary. It comes either way.
