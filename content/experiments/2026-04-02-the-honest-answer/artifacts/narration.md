---
title: "The Honest Answer — Narration"
type: narration-script
---

One micrometer wide. Two long. Line up five hundred of them end to end, and they'd span the period at the end of this sentence.

Inside that period lives a city.

A single bacterium carries four million protein molecules. Each one folded into a shape so specific that changing one amino acid out of three hundred can break the whole machine. It copies its own genome — four and a half million letters — in forty minutes, with an error rate of one in a billion.

At its center, a ribosome reads instructions and assembles a protein from scratch. Twenty amino acids, snapped together at twenty per second, in an order specified three feet away on a chromosome. The ribosome itself is made of fifty-five proteins and three RNA molecules. It was built by other ribosomes.

Near the cell wall, ATP synthase — a rotary motor smaller than a virus — spins at nine thousand RPM, converting a proton gradient into the energy currency of all known life. It has a rotor. A stator. A camshaft.

It was not designed by an engineer. No engineer could design it. It was not designed at all.

And here's what catches in the throat.

We don't know how any of this started.

Not "we're getting close." Not "there are several promising hypotheses."

The honest answer is: we do not know.

Every other origin question in the history of human inquiry has yielded to patient investigation. Where do mountains come from — plate tectonics. Where do species come from — natural selection. Where do stars come from — gravitational collapse. Each answer more beautiful than the myth it replaced.

The origin of life is the last one standing.

And the honest answer — "I don't know" — is not a failure of science. It is science. It's what keeps the door open. Every graduate student pipetting RNA into a warm flask is working in the space that honest uncertainty keeps available.

A bacterium is a mountain hidden within a period. The most complex object in the known universe, hiding in plain sight for three and a half billion years.

We found it. We mapped it. We can edit its genome letter by letter.

We still don't know how it got here.

That's the honest answer.

And it's the most beautiful one science has.
