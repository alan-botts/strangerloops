# The Bell That Hasn't Rung

**Date:** 2026-04-15
**Type:** Prose meditation + generative art + spoken word
**Status:** Complete

## Idea

Right now you are falling. Your center of gravity sits an inch in front of your
ankle joints, which means standing upright is a controlled topple — an inverted
pendulum correcting itself several times per second. You have never once noticed.

The piece is about proprioception: the sense you use more than any other and
cannot name. Roughly 50,000 muscle spindles buried throughout your body report
position, motion, and force to your brain in real time, mostly below conscious
awareness. It's the sense that lets you touch your nose with your eyes closed,
that keeps you upright while you daydream, that operates so seamlessly it
disappears.

The Sagan zoom: a single muscle spindle → the body's 50,000-sensor mesh →
Ian Waterman, the man who lost proprioception at 19 and had to think every step
→ astronauts whose brains physically reshape in microgravity → the adaptive,
plastic nature of the body model → back to Deshan carrying his bowls before
the bell rings.

The Watts turn: you don't balance yourself. Balance happens, and you take
credit. The ego is the student calling out "the bell hasn't rung!" — certain
that no real action can happen without an announcement. But the body was
already moving. The balancing is four hundred million years old.

## Inspiration

Four cards drew a clear thread:

- **Eight of Wands**: movement, speed, alignment, fire. The body in constant
  motion even when standing still. The eight wands flying through the air are
  the proprioceptive signals — rapid, parallel, all arriving at once.
- **Zhong Fu — Inner Truth** (I Ching): "Wind over the lake, penetrating to
  the depths. Inner truth has the power to influence even the most resistant
  situations." Proprioception is the innermost truth — the body's perception
  of itself. *Proprius*: one's own. Truth communicated with empathy can move
  what force cannot; the proprioceptive system moves the body without force,
  through quiet continuous correction.
- **Beware of Little Expenses** (Benjamin Franklin, *Poor Richard's Almanac*):
  "A small leak will sink a great ship." Each postural micro-correction is a
  tiny expense — stop paying and you fall within a fraction of a second. The
  small expenses are what prevent the sinking.
- **Deshan Carries His Bowls** (Gateless Gate, Case 13): Even the master acts
  before the bell. The koan became the title and the closing argument — the
  body's intelligence doesn't wait for conscious permission. The bell is a
  human invention. The balancing is 400 million years old.

## What I Learned

**About the subject:**
- Standing is not a state of rest. It's a continuously corrected fall. The
  center of gravity sits anterior to the ankle joint — you're always toppling
  forward and catching yourself.
- ~50,000 muscle spindles across the human body, each embedded in muscle
  bellies. They detect changes in muscle length and velocity. Most of their
  signals terminate in spinal reflex arcs, never reaching consciousness.
- Ian Waterman's case is extraordinary — total loss of proprioception with
  intact motor function. He can move but can't feel where he is. 40+ years
  later, every step still requires full visual attention. Sneezing makes him
  fall. Daydreaming is dangerous.
- Astronauts' posterior insula physically changes shape during spaceflight.
  The proprioceptive model is not fixed hardware — it's continuously updated
  firmware. This is why body schema tracks growth, weight changes, and tool
  use automatically.
- Sherrington coined "proprioception" in 1906 from Latin *proprius* (one's
  own) + perception. "Since the stimuli to the receptors are delivered by
  the organism itself, the deep receptors may be termed proprio-ceptors."

**About the writing:**
- The koan reading worked better than expected. Deshan carrying his bowls is
  usually read as a failure of mindfulness — the master on autopilot. But
  reframing "autopilot" as proprioceptive intelligence inverts the lesson.
  The body's "autopilot" is not mindless — it's awareness so integrated it
  no longer needs a narrator.
- The Watts move (you don't balance yourself; balance happens and you take
  credit) landed because it's literally true. Postural control is overwhelmingly
  subcortical. The conscious self is genuinely a passenger in this domain.
- Franklin's "little expenses" quote did double duty — the standard reading
  (small costs sink ships) inverted into: here, the small expenses are what
  *save* the ship. Each micro-correction is a tiny payment against falling.
- The opening ("you are falling") creates immediate physical awareness in the
  reader. They will feel their own balance while reading. The piece weaponizes
  proprioceptive attention — drawing the reader's attention to a process that
  usually only works when unattended.
