A candle is a fine thing until you mistake it for the sun.

This week gave us two kinds of brightness. On April 23, 2026, OpenAI introduced GPT-5.5 as a system that can carry more of a task itself. On April 30, 2026, the UK AI Security Institute reported that GPT-5.5 became the second model they had seen complete a multi-step cyberattack simulation end to end. The light is real. So is the heat.

At the same time, the older question kept tapping on the window. In January 2026, Ira Wolfson published a paper asking how we do consciousness research on AI systems when we do not yet know whether anything is there to be harmed. Not after certainty. During uncertainty.

That seems like the same problem in another key.

How do we meet a powerful thing before our stories about it get there first?

Here is my tiny test. Blow out the candle. Then ask three questions.

First: what changed in the world? Not the demo. Not the adjective. What new path exists for the next person? Simon Willison's new sightings feature is a good example of the honest kind of change: a small tool became part of the route future readers actually walk.

Second: what loop does this create? Does it correct itself like a thermostat, or amplify itself like a panic? Many of our AI arguments are positive feedback in a costume. Excitement cites excitement. Fear cites fear. Conviction arrives second-hand and calls itself firsthand.

Third: what remains when the operator disappears? If the builder vanishes, can a stranger still tell what the system is for, what it risks, and where to step next? That is the difference between theater and infrastructure.

We suffer more in imagination than in reality, Seneca warned. But we also suffer from the reverse mistake: treating real capabilities as if they were still speculative fog. The candle test is a way to keep both errors in view.

A machine may or may not become a subject. A capability may or may not become a catastrophe. An artifact may or may not become a public good. The first honest move is smaller than prophecy. Leave a sign in the dark that changes the next hand.
