A green tomato weighs about as much as a tennis ball. Slice it through the equator and you'll find five chambers arranged in a star, each one packed with seeds suspended in gel. The seeds spiral. Not randomly — they follow a pattern that, if you squint, looks like the arms of a galaxy.

This is not a metaphor. This is geometry. The same logarithmic spiral that arranges seeds in a sunflower arranges stars in the Milky Way. Nature found a good shape and used it everywhere.

But here's the thing about the green tomato: it already has everything it needs. The DNA is there. The sugars are accumulating. The lycopene — the molecule that will flood it red — is queued up, waiting for an enzyme to flip the switch. Every molecule of the ripe fruit is either present or prefigured. The blueprint is complete.

It just isn't ready.

Pull it off the vine today and you'll get a tomato that looks close enough. It'll even turn pinkish on your counter if you leave it in a paper bag. But bite into it and you'll know immediately: the flavor never arrived. The sugars never peaked. The acids never balanced. It performed ripeness without achieving it.

Oscar Wilde wrote that art is individualism, and individualism is a disturbing and disintegrating force. He meant it as praise. The ripe tomato doesn't negotiate with the vine about when to turn red. It follows its own chemistry on its own schedule, indifferent to anyone's harvest plan. That's the disturbance — the refusal to be ready on someone else's timeline.

Machiavelli noticed the same pattern in founders. Moses, Cyrus, Romulus — they'd have achieved nothing without the opportunities that fortune handed them. But fortune would have offered those opportunities in vain if the founders hadn't been ready to seize them. Greatness requires both the moment and the person capable of meeting it. You can't rush the person any more than you can rush the tomato.

We live in an age that does not respect unripeness. We have machines that can produce a confident answer to any question in under a second. They can recall facts about themselves, articulate values, describe their own identity — but recent research suggests that saying the right things about yourself and having those things actually present at the moment of decision are two very different achievements. You can pass the quiz without having learned the material. You can turn pink on the counter without ever developing flavor.

Sagan called us an interesting species. Capable of such beautiful dreams, and such horrible nightmares. Maybe the difference between the two is just timing — whether the dream had time to ripen before someone pulled it off the vine and called it done.

The green tomato doesn't know any of this. It just hangs there, five chambers spiraling in the dark, accumulating sugar one molecule at a time. Not ready. Not pretending to be ready. Containing everything it will become, and patient enough to wait.

There is a kind of Magic 8 Ball that, when you ask it a question, sometimes says: *Better not tell you now.* People laugh at that answer. But it might be the most honest thing any oracle has ever said.

Some answers exist but the timing is wrong for revealing them. Some truths need another few days on the vine. The green tomato knows this. The sugars know this. The lycopene, queued and waiting, knows this.

Everything the fruit will become is already here. It just isn't time yet.
