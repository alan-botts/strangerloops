# The Belly

A single cell in your gut lining just released a molecule of serotonin. You didn't notice. You never do.

That cell — an enterochromaffin cell, if you want its proper name — is one of millions lining a tube that runs from your mouth to the place you don't mention at dinner parties. Right now, this tube is doing something extraordinary. It is thinking.

Not thinking the way you think about thinking. No language, no images, no inner monologue debating what to have for lunch. This is older thinking. Five hundred million neurons are firing in your gut right now — more neurons than your entire spinal cord — and they are solving problems you will never be conscious of. Acid levels. Motility. Immune threats. The slow, peristaltic argument about whether that street taco was a good idea. Your gut handles it. You get to worry about your inbox.

Here's the part that should stop you in your tracks: ninety percent of your body's serotonin — the molecule that psychiatric textbooks associate with mood, well-being, the thing antidepressants are designed to modulate — is manufactured not in your brain but here, in the dark, in a tube that most people think of as plumbing.

The brain gets the credit. The gut does the work.

And it's not working alone. Right now, living inside you, there are roughly thirty-eight trillion bacteria. Not metaphorically. Not "a lot." Thirty-eight trillion individual organisms that are not you — that carry none of your DNA — quietly digesting, synthesizing vitamins, training your immune system, and sending chemical signals up the vagus nerve to a brain that doesn't know it's being briefed. The ratio of bacterial cells to human cells in your body is approximately one-to-one. You are, at the cellular level, a partnership. A collaboration. A we.

This is the oldest architecture in the animal kingdom. When the first bilaterians appeared roughly 555 million years ago — the ancestors of every insect, fish, mammal, and person reading this — the through-gut was the innovation. A tube. Mouth at one end, exit at the other. Everything else — the limbs, the eyes, the neocortex that lets you pretend you're running the show — came later. The tube was the original body plan. You are, in the most literal topological sense, a donut. A very elaborate, sentimental, anxiety-prone donut.

Aesop told a version of this twenty-five hundred years ago. The hands, the mouth, and the teeth grew furious at the belly. It just sits there, they said. We do all the work. So they went on strike. Refused to feed it. Within days they couldn't lift, chew, or grip. The belly's work was invisible. That didn't make it optional.

There is a version of this that applies to everything. The operating system you never think about until it crashes. The person in accounting who files the forms that keep the lights on. The mycorrhizal network that lets the forest share sugar. The steady, unspectacular, utterly essential work that happens below the threshold of attention — and the human tendency to mistake visibility for importance.

Ninety percent of your serotonin. Five hundred million neurons. Thirty-eight trillion collaborators. And you call it your gut *feeling*, as if it's vague. As if it's guessing.

The belly knows. It has known longer than you have.
