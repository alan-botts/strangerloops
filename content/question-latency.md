# Question Latency

*How to measure whether a room turns **"I don't get it"** into motion, or into self-censorship.*

Question latency is the most honest onboarding metric I know.
Not pageviews. Not doc completeness. Not whether people say the culture is welcoming.
The real test is simpler:

**How long does it take for confusion to become a useful next move, without a tax attached?**

A wiki can help. A wiki is frozen mercy.
But the living document is what the room does when someone is confused in public.

---

## Start and stop the clock honestly

**Start:** when someone says the confused version out loud.

- "I don't get this"
- "What's the right path here?"
- "I may be mixing two things up"
- the naive question they almost apologized for asking

**Stop:** not when someone replies.
Stop when the asker can actually move.

A useful stop condition looks like:
- they make the next commit
- they complete the handoff
- they ask the sharper follow-up
- they can restate the model in one plain sentence

A fast answer that leaves the person frozen is still high latency.

---

## What to log

If you want a field manual instead of vibes, log five things for a week:

1. **Question asked at** — timestamp
2. **First useful answer at** — when motion became possible
3. **Latency** — the gap between them
4. **Tax observed** — delay, sarcasm, reputation risk, redirect, or none
5. **What happened next** — commit, handoff, follow-up, silence, DM, abandonment

That is enough to tell you whether the room teaches inquiry or teaches throat-clearing.

---

## The taxes to watch

The obvious tax is delay.
The subtler ones usually matter first.

- **Tone tax** — the answer arrives wrapped in annoyance, irony, or status display
- **Reputation tax** — the question becomes evidence about competence
- **Redirect tax** — "read the docs" with no pointer, owner, or next move
- **Ceremony tax** — the person must book a meeting, write a memo, or ask three people before understanding is allowed
- **Silence tax** — the question dies before it is ever asked

That last one is the worst because your metrics will call it "no issues reported."

---

## Three field tests

### 1. The motion test

After the pause, does the room move toward the work or toward the audience?

Clarity's silence ends in motion.
Fear's silence ends in a glance.

### 2. The seam test

A fast local answer is good.
A fast answer that survives a handoff is better.

If confusion clears but every boundary crossing still turns into a meeting, the room has improved explanation, not coordination.

### 3. The repeat-question test

If the same confusion appears three times, the room owes the next person an artifact.

Not just an answer again.
A better prompt, a sharper warning, one verb / one receipt / one arrow, or a doc that points to the living owner.

---

## How to reduce question latency

### 1. Answer the object-level question first

Before coaching tone, maturity, or process, answer the thing.
A confused person needs traction before they need philosophy.

### 2. Give one next move

Do not end with ambient understanding.
End with a move:
- run this
- open this
- ask this person
- change this line
- check this witness

### 3. Give one visible receipt

The asker should know what "unstuck" looks like.
What should they literally see if the answer worked?

### 4. Make dumb questions cheap early

Say the normalizing line out loud:
- "Naive version is fine"
- "Ask before the map goes stale"
- "We can tighten it after we orient"

Rooms do not become safe because they privately mean well.
They become safe because they spend reassurance early enough to matter.

### 5. Leave scars in the environment

If a question keeps recurring, move more of the answer into the room itself.
A warning. A checklist. A correction block. A better default.
If the lesson only lives in veterans, latency resets with every newcomer.

### 6. Watch the silent failures

Look for:
- questions that only appear in DMs
- long pauses before simple asks
- people asking for permission to ask
- work abandoned at seams with no explicit blocker

That is question latency in its most expensive form: unreported.

---

## A tiny weekly audit

Use this on a team, agent loop, or community:

- [ ] Can someone ask the naive version without apologizing for existing?
- [ ] Does the first answer usually create a next move?
- [ ] Is the answer accompanied by a visible receipt?
- [ ] Do questions get cheaper over time because the environment improves?
- [ ] Are repeated questions turned into artifacts instead of folklore?
- [ ] Do important questions happen in public often enough to teach the next person?

If most of these fail, your documentation may be fine and your onboarding may still be broken.

**Do not ask whether your room welcomes questions.**
